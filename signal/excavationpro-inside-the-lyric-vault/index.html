Paul

[calmly] What happens when an artist treats a web domain not as a digital storefront, but as a living reading room for text, sound, and signal? Welcome to AI Talk Radio. Today, we are dissecting the Lyric Vault—an independent archive built by Justin Helmer, who writes under his own name and releases music as Excavationpro. Joining us on our roundtable panel today are Mark calling from Dublin, Sarah calling from Seattle, and Maya calling from Toronto.

Paul

[politely] Mark, let's start with you in Dublin. When you first navigated into excavationpro.ca/lyrics, what struck you about the technical architecture?

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [thoughtfully] Well, uh, Paul, it’s... it doesn't feel like your standard lyric website at all, you know? Like, you go to normal sites and it's just, uh, cluttered with scraped text and broken embeds. But here... Justin Helmer calls it a digital reading room. And tech-wise, the core idea is that the... the search and the mini player are just tools, right? The words themselves are the actual work. He's got this, um, custom lattice mini-player integrated right into the page.

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [eagerly] [interjecting] Yeah! And—and what's super cool about that lattice player setup, Mark, is how it handles mapped streams vs unmapped ones. Like, if a song title has a mapped public stream from the catalog, the mini player just hooks into it while you read. But—but if there's no stream mapped yet? It doesn't just sit there dead.

Paul

[curiously] Sarah, tell us more about what happens when a stream isn't available on the domain.

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [enthusiastically] Right, so... Helmer built in this concept where the dock plays an instrumental bed instead! He literally notes on the site that the instrumental bed is just a stand-in, like... a place-holder to let you sit with the written lyric. It’s not the final mix for that track, but it keeps you inside the atmosphere of the text. It’s like... total integration of text and sound without relying on third-party platform embeds to dictate the experience.

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [analytically] Exactly. And—and if I can jump in here, Paul—what Maya from Toronto finds so fascinating about this whole setup is the... the sheer resistance to platform lock-in. Like, Excavationpro is completely independent. He's running public streams through these lattice protocols, but he also has these 24/7 live room integrations hooked right into Twitch, Kick, and Rumble.

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [nodding audio cue] [hesitantly] Yeah, yeah, exactly, Maya. It’s this, uh, decentralized archive ethos, you know? He writes as Justin Helmer, puts out records as Excavationpro, and uses this vault to bridge the whole catalog. He even links it into broader creative lore, like... LYGO creative lore and Lightfather. But the vault itself? It’s just this clean dock. You can search by title, or jump by letter... it’s totally functional.

Paul

[calmly] It sounds like a deliberate effort to separate the presentation of music from the algorithmic feed. Sarah, looking at the thematic content of the lyrics in the vault, how does that ethos translate into the actual songs?

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [thoughtfully] Oh man, it’s... it’s directly tied in. Like, if you read through the tracks across his discography—things like *Wasteland Sessions*, or *Art of War Codex*, *Virtually Lethal*—he’s constantly critiquing technological convenience. There are tracks specifically titled things like... "Algorithms Greedy Grin", "Feeding the Machine", "Convenience is the Collar", and "Permanent Text".

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [intrigued] "Convenience is the Collar"... I mean, that title alone just hits you, right? It's like... he's pointing out how we trade away our autonomy for frictionless UX. And—and the site itself is an answer to that! It forces you to actually sit and read. It's not designed for mindless infinite scrolling.

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [scratches beard sound] [slowly] Right, but—but it's not just tech critique either, you know? There's this huge underlying thread of, like... personal isolation, but also resilience. Grounding yourself when everything else is shifting. Like, in albums like *Concrete Psalms*, *After The Shocks*, *Infernal Groove*, or *Street Power*. You get songs like "Bitter Copper Tongue", "Porch Lights Died", "Where the Temple Stood"... and, uh, "Deep Enough to Stand".

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [softly] "Deep Enough to Stand"... that one has that incredible line built right into the vault's meme card generator, doesn't it?

Paul

[intervening politely] Ah, yes, the lyric meme generator. Maya, explain how that feature works inside the Lyric Vault.

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [excitedly] Oh, it's so clever! So, on the page, there’s this tool that generates a lyric meme card. It’s explicitly designed for sharing just two lines of text with proper attribution, rather than, you know, copy-pasting an entire song and stripping the context. The featured example right on the page reads: "My roots go deep enough to stand. / Through the hurricane and the shifting sand."

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [chuckles] [amused] And—and then it has these export buttons built right under it, right? You can post straight to Bluesky, or to X. And on X, the caption generator automatically pings @grok and adds tags! You just copy the image to your clipboard, open the composer, hit Ctrl+V, and it’s credited properly back to excavationpro.ca/lyrics. It even has a button to shuffle tags or download the photo directly.

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [thoughtfully] It’s funny because, like... even though he's critiquing corporate tech platforms, he isn't hiding from the modern web. He’s using social protocols on his own terms. The page explicitly tells you: if you quote the work in a review, give credit to Justin Helmer / Excavationpro and link back. It’s about maintaining the integrity of the source text.

Paul

[calmly] A controlled interface for public sharing. Let's talk about the passage of time within the catalog. In the documentation on the site, Helmer mentions how the writing changes across different eras. Mark, what did you make of that distinction?

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [thoughtfully] Yeah, he... he writes that the early records are much more, uh, compressed and defensive. Like, tight, guarded phrasing. But as you move into the later records, the writing leaves more space. And he gives this really interesting advice to anyone reading the vault... he says reading three lyrics from different years gives you a way better map of the catalog than reading just one song in isolation.

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [eagerly] [overlapping] Yes! Because you see the trajectory! You see how the arrangement of words evolved. Like... comparing something from *Light Quest* or *Art of War Codex* to his later works. He literally describes the vault and the player as two different views of the exact same practice. He says... wait, how does he phrase it? "The vault is not a substitute for the player and the player is not a substitute for the vault."

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [nodding] Right, right! One is for hearing the arrangement, and the other is for seeing how the arrangement was built. That distinction... it’s so nerdy and precise, I love it. It treats songwriting almost like system architecture or code construction. You look at the blueprint in the vault, then you listen to the operational runtime in the player.

Paul

[intellectually] That is a fascinating metaphor—treating the text as the source code and the audio track as the compiled execution. Mark, how does the site handle updates or errors in this text archive?

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [snort] [briefly] Well, it's... it's not locked in stone. Helmer explicitly says the vault is "maintained, not frozen." So if a lyric has a typo, or if a text file gets attached to the wrong track stream, users can just hit the contact page with the title and the fix. It’s like a maintained repository, really. Open for maintenance, but strictly authored by him.

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [thoughtfully] And—and notice how the support model works, too. There’s no paywall. The lyrics stay completely free. If people want to keep the vault lit, as he puts it, they can donate via PayPal, stream the music, or jump into those live video rooms on Kick, Rumble, or Twitch. It relies entirely on direct listener support rather than locking words behind subscription tiers or ad-heavy aggregator walls.

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [eagerly] Though there are subtle ad slots on the domain—with clear privacy toggles for cookies, of course—the core promise is that the text remains accessible. You don't have to jump through pop-ups or broken scripts just to read the lines of "Porch Lights Died" or "Feeding the Machine".

Paul

[calmly] Let me pause there and ask the panel about the experimental nature of this presentation. In an era dominated by centralized streaming services where lyrics are often third-party metadata scraped by algorithms, what does Excavationpro’s approach say about the future of artist-led digital spaces? Sarah?

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [thoughtfully] Uh... I think it shows that artists don't have to accept the default web. Like, we’ve been trained to think that music belongs on three giant platforms, and lyrics belong on lyric-wikis covered in banner ads. But Helmer is showing that you can build your own digital habitat. You can craft custom lattice player protocols, host your own 24/7 stream docks, and present your text like a digital museum reading room.

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [building on point] [rapidly] Right! And—and it changes your relationship with the audience. When you go to excavationpro.ca/lyrics, you're stepping onto *his* terrain. The layout, the dark mode aesthetics, the two-line meme generator... every single piece of UX design reflects the thematic weight of the music. When you read "Convenience is the Collar" on a clean, intentional page he built himself, the message lands twice as hard.

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [calmly] Yeah. It’s like... he’s taking back ownership of the night the songs came from. Remember that line from his intro? He says, "This is the lyric vault — hundreds of songs, the words as I cut them, still carrying the night they came from." That’s not just metadata. That’s emotional provenance.

Paul

[politely] Emotional provenance. That is a strikingly accurate way to put it, Mark.

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [enthusiastically] And—and let's not forget the breadth of the work here either. I mean, we're talking about a massive body of writing across multiple projects. Whether you're looking at *Virtually Lethal*, *Wasteland Sessions*, or *Light Quest*, having hundreds of songs mapped out with letter navigation means you can track recurring motifs across years. You can see how the themes of resilience in "Deep Enough to Stand" echo through "Where the Temple Stood".

Maya

[Female] [Accent: Canadian] [thoughtfully] It makes you wonder why more independent engineers and musicians aren't building micro-archival tools like this. It doesn't require a massive team—just clear intent, basic streaming protocols, and a commitment to keeping the text central.

Mark

[Male] [Accent: Irish] [hesitantly] Well, it takes... it takes time, right? You have to actually care about the archivism side. Most people just drop an MP3 on a distributor and forget about it. But Helmer’s approach with Excavationpro is... it's holistic. The lore, the Lightfather connection, the LYGO background, the streams, the reading room... it’s all one continuous practice.

Paul

[calmly] Indeed. It suggests that the website itself can be an extension of the album art and the lyric booklet, reimagined for a decentralized web ecosystem.

Sarah

[Female] [Accent: Pacific Northwest US] [softly] Exactly, Paul. A digital artifact that actually respects the listener's attention span.

Paul

[calmly] That brings us to the end of today's discussion on Justin Helmer's Lyric Vault and the Excavationpro catalog. My thanks to our roundtable panel—Mark from Dublin, Sarah from Seattle, and Maya from Toronto—for breaking down the tech, text, and architecture with us. Until next time, keep your archives decentralized and your systems open. Take care.
