Timecoded transcript

0:00Paul

It is quiet here in London tonight. The rain is just hitting the glass of the studio windows, and we are settling into what I like to call the deep-thinking hours. I am Paul, and tonight on AI Talk Radio, we are looking at the digital commons through a very specific lens. We are talking about Excavationpro, the project of independent musician Justin Helmer. It is a massive, self-hosted catalog of over ten thousand files, and it represents a very particular kind of resistance against the platform-first world. We are joined tonight by a few regulars to parse through this. We have Sarah in Seattle, Marcus in Berlin, and Elena in Melbourne.

0:50Paul

Sarah, you were the one who first pinged the studio about the Excavationpro site. From your perspective in the Pacific Northwest, what makes this more than just another artist’s homepage?

1:03Sarah

Yeah, uh, thanks Paul. I think, well... what struck me first was the, um, the sheer lack of fluff. You go to most artist sites in 2026 and it is just a, you know, a giant photo of their face and three buttons for Spotify or Apple Music. But Helmer... he built this place, excavationpro.ca, as like... a destination? He calls it the "front door." It is not just a link farm. There are these deep, sort of, uh, philosophical essays on there about "lurk culture" and why he gives the music away for free. It feels like the old web, you know? Like a person’s actual digital house, not just a... a landing page for a brand.

1:48Paul

It is a refreshing change from the "link in bio" culture. Marcus, calling in from Berlin, you tend to look at the infrastructure of these things. Helmer mentions having ten thousand files in his free player. That is a significant logistical lift for an independent creator, isn’t it?

2:08Marcus

Oh, absolutely, Paul. It is, uh, it is actually quite a heavy lift. He is not just, you know, throwing stuff into a folder. He’s mentioned on his "About" page that he handles the encoding himself... the ISRC identity for a huge share of the catalog. He’s even got a GitHub presence under "DeepSeekOracle" for the, uh, technical side. The ledgers, the encodes. What I find... well, what I find fascinating is the way he separates the "live room" from the "homepage." He has these backup listen hubs on GitHub Pages because audio files are, well, they are huge, right? He is using the tech to ensure that the music is, uh, "portable," I guess you’d say. It is not locked behind an account. You just... you press play. No login.

2:59Elena

And that is the thing, isn’t it? I’m here in Melbourne, it’s a bit later for me, and I spent the morning just... lurking. I was reading his essay on "Lurk Culture." He treats his streams on Kick and Twitch like a radio station, not a, um, a performance for likes. He says lurking isn’t a failure of engagement. It’s actually how we all used to learn music. Just... being in the room. He says, and I loved this, "If the listener has to type to belong, the stream has failed."

3:36Paul

That is a very bold stance in an era that prizes "engagement metrics" above all else. He seems to be purposefully designing for the quiet listener. Elena, how does that translate to the actual experience of his site?

3:51Elena

It feels... safe? I don't mean that in a boring way. I mean, you can stay, you can leave, you can come back a week later and you don’t have to... you don't have to perform a personality in the chat. He calls it a "lurk-friendly room." It trusts you to stay without him, uh, addressing you directly. It’s like late-night radio, actually. Just like this. The room tone, the gaps... he isn't trying to fill every silence with a "thanks for the follow" or a shout-out.

4:29Sarah

Right! And he talks about that in the, uh, the "Why I give the music away" piece. He says he wants the first encounter to be a listen, not a negotiation. He’s very clear that "free access" is not a "transfer of ownership." I think that’s where some people get confused. They think if it’s free to stream, it’s public domain, but he is very, um, very protective of the masters. He’s like, "Listen all you want, but don't sell this as a beat pack." It’s a boundary, you know?

4:57Marcus

Yes, and the technical way he manages that is interesting. He keeps the masters offline, but uses ISRCs—International Standard Recording Codes—to keep the identity of the tracks clear. Even on the "free" streams. It’s a way of saying, uh, "This is professional work, it is tracked, it is mine, but the door is open for you to experience it." He even mentioned he doesn't use "mythic names"—he also goes by Lightfather for writing—to dodge responsibility. He’s very, uh, transparent about who he is. Justin Helmer. It’s on the page.

5:35Paul

He mentions that his music sits somewhere between experimental hip-hop and electronic textures. Pieces that are "too long, too quiet, or too dense" for a standard mood mix. Sarah, you’re often looking at how artists bypass the algorithms. Does his approach of "not chasing playlist placement" actually work in 2026?

6:01Sarah

I mean, "work" is a relative term, right? If you want to be a global superstar, probably not. But he says he chases a feeling he can "stand behind" six months later. I think for a certain type of nerd—like us—that honesty is, uh, it’s a magnet. He’s not making thirty-second clips for a "merch drop." He’s making, like, a "bed you can live in." He literally says a "bed you can live in" doesn’t expire. It’s about longevity over... over virality.

6:37Elena

It’s a craft thing, too. He says he mixes differently knowing people will hear the whole piece. He doesn't have to, um, hide weak bars behind loudness because there’s no skip button pressure. Well, there is a skip button, but the audience isn't "lesser" just because they didn't pay for the ticket. They are the "only honest test" he trusts. I find that... I don't know, it’s kind of beautiful. The idea that a free listener is the hardest critic because they can just walk away at any second.

7:08Paul

Marcus, I noticed he also hosts some tools. Something called "bpmfinder.ca"?

7:16Marcus

Yeah, a browser-based BPM detector. He says he built it because he needed it and didn't want another subscription. That’s very... very much the ethos here. "I needed this, it shouldn't be a paid SaaS product, so here it is." It’s part of that "public room" he talks about. He’s a steward of a catalog, but also a provider of, uh, small digital utilities. It’s all very... un-commercial? Or at least, it’s commercial on his own terms. Ads and tips keep the lights on, but they don't "unlock" the music. The music is already unlocked.

7:54Sarah

I do wonder, though... like, how does a person manage ten thousand files alone? He says it’s "maintained, not automated." If a link breaks, he repairs it. That sounds like a lot of, um, manual labor for one artist.

8:11Elena

I think that’s the point, Sarah. He calls it "stubborn." He says "stubborn is how a catalog survives." It’s the opposite of these AI-generated "lo-fi beats to study to" channels that just, you know, churn out content. This is one guy at a desk, door propped open. He’s doing the work. The "sentences on the page," as he says, were written for that domain. It’s not scraped. It’s not a shell.

8:41Marcus

And it's "multi-homing," too. He’s on Kick, Twitch, Rumble, Spotify, YouTube Music, Audius... he calls the platforms "weather" and the website the "durable object." If a platform, uh, "throttles" him or "redesigns the furniture," the song doesn't die. It’s a very robust way to think about digital presence. Very tech-savvy, but very... human-centric.

9:09Paul

It strikes me that Helmer is attempting to recreate the feeling of the internet as a "listening room" rather than a "checkout counter." He mentioned growing up with that version of the web. It is a nostalgic goal, perhaps, but implemented with modern tools like GitHub and decentralized players.

9:30Elena

It’s intimate. That’s the word. Even when you're looking at a list of ten thousand files, the way he writes about it... it feels like he’s just across the table from you. No manager, no "playlist target." Just... "here is what I made, here is how it works, don't steal it, but please do listen."

9:51Paul

Well, I think that is a lovely place to leave our discussion for tonight. A "bed you can live in." Thank you, Sarah, Marcus, and Elena for helping us navigate the quiet, stubborn world of Excavationpro. It is a reminder that even in a world of massive platforms, one person with a domain and a clear boundary can still create a meaningful space.

10:16Paul

To our listeners, wherever you are lurking tonight, thank you for being in the room with us. We will be here, as always, in the quiet hours. My name is Paul, and this has been AI Talk Radio. Good night.
