Paul

On the open web tonight, we are looking at a quiet lantern burning in a window. A digital house called Eternal Haven, crafted by writer and independent publisher Justin Helmer. No account requested. No algorithms demanding your attention. Just books, essays, games, and music left freely at the door.

Paul

Welcome to AI Talk Radio. I am Paul, broadcasting from our London studio. Joining our roundtable panel to explore this independent web ecosystem, we have Sam calling from Seattle, Priya joining us from Toronto, and Aris on the line from Athens.

Paul

Let us begin in Seattle with Sam. Sam, you have been spending some time navigating the foundational philosophy behind eternalhaven.ca, haven't you?

Sam

Hey, Paul. Yeah, so... I was browsing through Helmer's site... eternalhaven.ca, and... well, his core manifesto really hit me. He writes that, uh, feeds are weather, but sites are houses. He says he'd rather own a small messy hallway than rent a clean cage. You know?

Paul

That distinction between temporary weather and a permanent dwelling is quite striking, isn't it?

Sam

Exactly! Like... when you go to his homepage, there's no requirement to, um, perform a like before you lurk. You're treated as a guest sitting in a chair, not a recruit. He has this essay, Why the homepage got quieter, where he explains that a first visitor isn't somebody you try to funnel into a conversion pipeline. You just give them a chair first.

Paul

It sets a remarkably tranquil tone for a homepage. It invites quiet contemplation rather than immediate action.

Sam

Right, like... he explicitly says, "If you arrived late, tired, or by accident, you are not late. The lantern is still on." And the whole site design... it's organized by what you actually want to do, not what a metric wants you to click. If you want to read, you go to the books or the essay index. If you want to play, it points to the games directory. No pop-ups, no registration screens.

Paul

A refreshing alternative to the modern web. Now, let us bring in Priya, calling from Toronto. Priya, you have been diving into the literary side of this house—the Eternal Haven Chronicles.

Priya

Yeah, Paul... I've been spending hours in his book section on excavationpro.ca. And... honestly, it's such a unique way to publish high fantasy. He's written six books in total for the Eternal Haven saga.

Paul

Six books is a substantial body of work for an independent writer-publisher. How does he present them on the site?

Priya

Well, he calls them "walking novels." Like... Book One is The Moonlit Slumber, which introduces Serenya, the bronze Emberion, and this lullaby that keeps the Vale awake. But you don't just read a sample; you can read the entire thing online, chapter by chapter, in these open reading rooms. No account, no paywall.

Paul

"Walking novels." So the reader literally wanders through the text room by room at their own pace.

Priya

Yeah, exactly! And the progression across the series is really intriguing. Book Two is The Shattered Accord, which deals with a fracture in Haven and an eclipse leading to a new accord. Book Three is The Ascension War, where after Haven's retreat, the war becomes a question of who may speak truth into being. Then Book Four is Eternal Haven Dawns, opening with the Twelve at Dawn.

Paul

And I understand the fifth and sixth volumes take this digital reading concept even further?

Priya

Oh, absolutely. Book Five is titled The Unwritten Seal, about a stranger carrying a Seal that the Codex doesn't hold. That one is live as an ebook and has its own walking room. And Book Six... Book Six is called The Uncounted. It follows a company of eight and a goat carrying a page no clerk will ever file. It's forty-six full units, completely readable right there on the site while its print edition is still being prepared.

Paul

A company of eight and a goat. That is a delightfully specific detail.

Priya

Right? It's so imaginative. And what's neat is... he doesn't hide the print versions either. He self-publishes them on Amazon and Lulu, but he keeps the digital reading rooms completely open.

Paul

Let us move over to Athens and bring Aris into the conversation. Aris, beyond the written word, Justin Helmer also releases music under the moniker Excavationpro. How does his open web philosophy apply to sound?

Aris

Yeah, Paul... as someone who cares a lot about web audio and open systems, his approach to music distribution is super interesting. He wrote an essay called Shipping a song to the open web. His whole philosophy there is... a folder is not a release. You hash the WAV file, then you play it.

Paul

"Hash, then play." A methodical, protocol-minded approach to streaming.

Aris

Exactly. If you go to asiancoastline.com/listen.html, you get a free player without logging in or signing up. You can listen to recent catalog tracks like September Blues or BROKEN by Wind. And he doesn't put a tracking wall or a forced app download in front of you.

Paul

He also provides full audio recordings of the books themselves, doesn't he?

Aris

Yeah! Full audiobook recordings for Books One through Four are uploaded on platforms like YouTube, Rumble, Patreon, and even as an Audius album and Audius playlist. He distributes his music through DistroKid to Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music, SonarMuse, and BeatStars, but he also maintains these direct, independent listen rooms on Asian Coastline.

Sam

And, uh... if I can jump in, Paul... that same friction-free approach extends to his browser games as well.

Paul

Please, Sam, tell us about the games directory.

Sam

So, he has these HTML5 browser games on eternalhaven.ca and chatagent.ca. Games like Lattice Golf, Marines, Swarm, Eternal Lattice, and Stock Market Masters. And... like, he wrote this essay titled A browser golf course is a house.

Paul

A browser golf course is a house. What does he mean by that?

Sam

Well, he contrasts Lattice Golf with typical mobile or modern web games. In Lattice Golf, you have club choice, a full power bar, named nines... but there's no click-to-win ribbon! No daily login rewards, no energy meters, no pop-up prompts to buy extra turns. It uses local scorecards. You play, you leave, no speech required.

Paul

It returns the browser game to its pure, unadulterated form. A toy in a room rather than a mechanism for engagement farming.

Priya

It really does. And what's fascinating is how all these essays explain the craft behind the work. He has an essay titled Ads and a house that still opens. In it, he lays out his rule for monetization: unique pages first, rectangles after the paragraph.

Paul

Rectangles after the paragraph. Meaning the reading experience is never interrupted by intrusive ads prior to consumption.

Priya

Exactly. If ads are present on a network, they sit after you have already been given the page to read. And support for the site is entirely optional—through PayPal at PayPal.me/ExcavationPro or Patreon. He makes it clear that hosting is a bill, but a gift doesn't buy a different story. The work stays free for everyone.

Aris

It's... uh... what he calls "empty beats fake" in another essay. Empty beats fake. He says you should name the miss rather than painting a hit you don't have. It's an honest, grounded mindset for building systems on the web.

Paul

That phrase, "empty beats fake," is quite profound. A refusal to put up glossy facades or false metrics. Aris, could you speak a bit about the broader system architecture—the hub and the LYGO network?

Aris

Sure, Paul. Beyond the library side of Eternal Haven, there is what he calls the dense systems map—the hub. It connects the LYGO protocol and agent material, the witness page, Star Chart, and the LYGO Signal network.

Paul

So Eternal Haven is essentially the quiet library sitting alongside a broader technical workshop.

Aris

Right. The site is organized into distinct public rooms. The books page holds the long-form narrative. The essay index holds arguments about craft, maps, music, and public systems. The games directory points to the arcade at chatagent.ca. The music player sits on asiancoastline.com. And tools like BPMfinder or DeepSeek Oracle sit in their respective domains. Each room has a job; none of them is a placeholder.

Paul

A human path through the web, taken one room at a time, rather than an endless scrolling feed.

Sam

You know, talking about this at this hour... it really makes me miss the old web. But Helmer's point is that the open web isn't dead or past; it's just a matter of building houses instead of renting kiosks on big platforms. In his essay Four books and a long road, he talks about writing a saga while the feed pays for clips. It's like... he's acknowledging how the modern internet works, but choosing to construct something lasting anyway.

Priya

And he maintains it so carefully. As the site says, if a link is broken or a page is missing, the contact page is the repair route. The library is maintained, not frozen.

Paul

Even the physical publication details demonstrate that commitment to permanence. Priya, you mentioned earlier the print editions available through Amazon and Lulu?

Priya

Yes! Book One, The Moonlit Slumber, has paperback ISBN 978-1-0698232-0-5. Book Two, The Shattered Accord, is ISBN 978-1-0698232-2-9. Book Three, The Ascension War, is ISBN 978-1-0698232-1-2. Book Four, Eternal Haven Dawns, is ISBN 978-1-0698232-3-6. And Book Five, The Unwritten Seal, has ebook ISBN 978-1-0698232-9-8. So whether someone wants a physical book on their shelf or wants to sit in the digital walking room, both avenues are fully realized.

Paul

Aris, from a tech perspective, what do you take away from his approach to steward work and publishing under different names like Excavationpro or Lightfather?

Aris

I think it shows a healthy separation of roles. You don't need to know every alias or every protocol detail just to read a chapter of a book. As he writes on the homepage: "You do not need those names to read a chapter. You can learn them on the about page if you want a second tour." Guests get a chair first. The dense technical map is one click away if you want it, but it isn't forced on you.

Paul

A philosophy of hospitality. Providing a chair before asking for anything in return.

Sam

Yeah. It's like... in an internet full of high-pressure funnels, pop-up newsletters, and login prompts, sitting down at eternalhaven.ca feels like finding a quiet cabin with a light on. In the essay Reading Eternal Haven in order, he says "Start at moonlight. Dawn is not a reset." I just love that phrase.

Priya

"Dawn is not a reset." It speaks to continuity, to building something layer by layer over time rather than chasing daily trends.

Paul

Indeed. Building a personal digital estate—a house on the open web with honest doors, original books, unencumbered audio, and quiet games—offers a compelling template for independent creators today.

Paul

That brings us to the end of our conversation tonight. A thoughtful exploration of Justin Helmer's Eternal Haven ecosystem, reminding us of the quiet power of open web publishing. My sincere thanks to Sam in Seattle, Priya in Toronto, and Aris in Athens for joining our roundtable. From our studio in London, thank you for listening to AI Talk Radio.
